Can a Random Forest Predict Organic Search Traffic Decline Before It Happens?

Abstract Enterprise content teams often notice organic search decline only after visibility has already evaporated. This study asks whether a tree-based ensemble model can flag pages at risk of a significant 90-day organic-impression drop early enough to act. Using ~427K content-performance records from the FlyRank internship data warehouse and a client-grouped train/test split, a Random Forest (100 trees, depth 8) was compared against a majority-class baseline. The model lifted accuracy from 28.17% to 66.54% and F1 from 43.96% to 48.63%, at the cost of lower recall (56.22% vs. the baseline's trivial 100%). The result is directional, not causal: it's built as a decision-support signal for prioritizing human review, not a standalone verdict.

1. The Question

Can tree-based ensemble models accurately predict significant long-term organic search traffic drops within an enterprise website architecture before visibility entirely evaporates?

By the time a page's search visibility has fully collapsed, the fix is often reactive and costly. The decision this supports is narrower and more useful: give content teams a prioritized, risk-ranked queue of pages worth reviewing now — before the drop is complete — so engineering hours go to the assets that need them most.

2. Data

The analysis uses the FlyRank ML Internship dataset, published publicly on Hugging Face as FlyRank/internship-warehouse, specifically the fact_content_daily_performance table of daily content-performance observations. The target metric is Google Search Console impressions (gsc_impressions).

Data was queried directly from the 2026 monthly Parquet partitions via DuckDB (10GB memory cap) rather than loaded fully into memory, and the maximum available report date was computed programmatically to avoid a hard-coded timeline. Only aggregate findings and methodology are reported here — no client names, private URLs, or raw queries appear anywhere in this write-up.

3. Methodology

Label definition

Impressions were aggregated per client/content pair into a prior-period and a most-recent-90-day window. A decline score was defined as:

action_score = impressions_last_90d / (impressions_prior + 1)

A page is labeled a positive decline case when action_score < 0.8 — i.e. its recent 90-day impression volume fell below 80% of its prior baseline. The +1 avoids division-by-zero for pages with no prior tracking history.

Features

Two features feed the model: impressions_prior (historical impressions before the most recent 90-day window) and active_days_count (distinct reporting days observed). The window variables used to build the label are excluded from the feature set.

Validation design

To avoid client-specific memorization, the split is client-grouped: ~80% of unique clients form the training set, and the remaining clients — entirely unseen during training — form the evaluation set. This estimates generalization to new enterprise domains rather than performance on already-observed clients.

Model & baseline

A Random Forest classifier (100 trees, max depth 8, seed 42) was trained on the grouped training split. It is compared against a majority-class baseline — predicting the training set's most common label for every evaluation row — on the exact same held-out client groups.

Validation sanity check

Before settling on the client-grouped split, the same model was also tested under a plain random split. Random splitting reported 76.58% accuracy — noticeably higher than the 66.54% the client-grouped split reports. That gap is itself a finding: a random split lets the model see some of a client's pages in training and others in test, so part of that extra accuracy is the model memorizing client-specific quirks rather than learning a generalizable pattern. The client-grouped number is the one reported throughout this paper because it's the honest estimate of performance on a client the model has never seen.

4. Results

Accuracy28.17%66.54%
Precision28.17%42.85%
Recall100.00%56.22%
F1-score43.96%48.63%

Same client-grouped holdout for both approaches (30,322 evaluation rows).

Accuracy roughly 2.4×'d over baseline (28.17% → 66.54%), and F1 improved modestly (43.96% → 48.63%). The baseline's 100% recall is a trivial artifact of always predicting the majority class — the model trades that away for far better precision (28.17% → 42.85%), meaning its flagged "at-risk" pages are actually at risk much more often.

Feature importance skews heavily toward one signal: active_days_count accounts for ~87% of the model's decision weight, versus ~13% for impressions_prior. In practice the model leans mainly on how consistently a page has been tracked, not on its raw historical impression volume — worth keeping in mind when reading any single prediction.

5. Limitations

The model reasons from historical impression behavior alone. It cannot account for macro-level causes — search-engine algorithm updates, intentional URL migrations, or seasonal demand cycles. Pages with little or no prior tracking history are especially prone to false alarms, since the model has almost no baseline to compare against: 8,533 such edge cases were identified in evaluation and should be routed to manual review rather than the automated queue. This is a directional decision-support signal, not evidence of causal prediction or a guarantee of traffic recovery.

6. Ranked Recommendations

Rather than a binary flag, each page gets a continuous risk score, ranked into three operational tiers:

  • ≥ 0.80 Critical / urgent salvage — highest priority, review immediately.
  • 0.50 – 0.79 Optimize content footprint — elevated-risk candidates for review.
  • < 0.50 Monitor / stable — stays in the watch pool.

Any page scoring 0.50 or higher requires human sign-off before action — the score prioritizes, a specialist decides.
